How to Know When Your Volvo Truck Rubber Spring Needs Replacing

Parts Trading Company · May 2026 · 6 min read

The rubber spring is a small, inexpensive part — but when it fails, it affects ride quality, driver fatigue, and the suspension geometry on your entire front axle. Here are the 5 signs fleet managers and drivers should watch for, plus a quick visual inspection guide.

Volvo's FH, FM, and FMX trucks use a rubber spring (part 20390836, also known as 1629553) on the front axle as part of the leaf spring suspension system. This component absorbs shock, dampens vibration, and keeps the axle geometry stable under load. Most operators never notice it — until it fails.

Rubber springs don't snap suddenly. They degrade gradually, which is both a good thing (you get warning signs) and a bad thing (the symptoms are easy to ignore). Here's what to look for.

5 Signs Your Rubber Spring Is Failing

  • 1

    Increased Cab Vibration at Highway Speed

    A healthy rubber spring absorbs small road inputs before they reach the chassis. When the rubber hardens or cracks, you'll feel a consistent vibration — especially between 60–90 km/h. Drivers often describe it as the steering wheel "buzzing" or the cab feeling harsher than usual. This is frequently the first sign, and it's easy to attribute to tyres or wheel balance — check the rubber spring before you spend money on balancing.

  • 2

    Clunking or Thudding Over Potholes and Speed Bumps

    A worn rubber spring loses its ability to cushion sharp impacts. What you'll hear is a dull metallic clunk as the suspension bottoms out without proper damping. This happens because the rubber compound has either compressed permanently (set) or cracked, leaving a metal-to-metal contact point in the suspension. Left unaddressed, this puts secondary stress on the leaf springs, U-bolts, and front axle beam.

  • 3

    Uneven Tyre Wear on the Front Axle

    Rubber springs contribute to maintaining the correct front axle geometry. When one side fails before the other — which is common — the camber and caster angles drift. The result is faster wear on the inner or outer edge of one front tyre. If you're seeing edge wear that can't be corrected by alignment alone, inspect both rubber springs before realigning.

  • 4

    Visible Cracking, Bulging, or Compression Set

    A physical inspection takes under 5 minutes and is the most reliable check. Look for rubber cracking along the surface (fine cracks are early-stage, deep cracks mean replacement is overdue), bulging on one side (indicates internal delamination), or a noticeably flattened profile compared to the other side. The steel insert should be fully embedded — if you can see the insert through the rubber, the part is compromised.

  • 5

    Pulling to One Side Under Braking

    When both rubber springs are in different states of wear, the front axle geometry is asymmetric. Under heavy braking, this manifests as a pull to one side — a safety concern on Indian highways where emergency braking is common. If a brake system check comes back clean and the truck still pulls, the suspension geometry is the next suspect. Compare the two rubber springs side by side during inspection.

Quick Visual Inspection Guide

Fleet Tip

Always replace rubber springs in pairs. Even if only one side shows visible damage, the other has experienced the same mileage and load cycles. Replacing one side creates an asymmetric suspension — which causes the problems you're trying to fix.

Replacement Tips

Replacing the rubber spring is a straightforward job for a competent workshop — it does not require axle removal on most Volvo FH/FM configurations. A few points to get right:

Check the spring seats. The mating surfaces on the axle and leaf spring where the rubber spring sits should be clean and flat. Any corrosion, burrs, or raised edges will cause the new spring to seat unevenly and fail early. Clean the seats with a wire brush before fitting.

Don't use grease. Rubber springs are designed to be fitted dry. Grease or silicone applied to the mating surfaces causes the rubber to slip under load, which leads to noise and premature wear. Clean and dry installation only.

Torque the U-bolts correctly. U-bolt torque affects how the rubber spring is compressed at rest. Over-tightening pre-loads the rubber spring beyond its design limit. Use the torque values from the Volvo workshop manual for the specific axle configuration — typically in the 350–500 Nm range for FH/FM front axles, but verify for your specific model year.

Do a ride height check after fitting. With the truck loaded to its normal operating weight, measure the front ride height on both sides. They should be within 5 mm of each other. If they're not, recheck the spring seating.

Related Parts to Check at the Same Time

When you're in the front suspension area, these parts are worth inspecting — they share the same wear environment and are cheap to replace proactively:

  • Leaf spring centre bolt — look for shear or movement under load
  • U-bolt and U-bolt plate — check for corrosion, thread condition, and plate flatness
  • Spring eye bushings — rubber or polyurethane; check for play and cracking
  • Shock absorber lower mount — the rubber bushing at the lower shock mount degrades at a similar rate to the rubber spring
