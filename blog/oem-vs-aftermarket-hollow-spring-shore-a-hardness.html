OEM vs Aftermarket Hollow Spring:
What Shore A Hardness Actually Tells You

Parts Trading Company·May 2026·7 min read

When you buy a hollow spring, you are buying a rubber compound specification. The part number tells you the geometry. Shore A hardness tells you whether it will actually work. This guide explains what the number means, what OEM spec requires, and how cheap aftermarket copies cut corners — and why that costs more in the end.

Fleet managers and workshop supervisors often evaluate hollow springs (rubber buffer mounts) purely on price and part number fit. That approach works until it doesn't — and when a low-grade rubber buffer fails early, the secondary costs (tyre wear, bearing damage, unplanned downtime) dwarf the saving on the part itself.

The single most useful number on a rubber suspension component specification sheet is its Shore A hardness rating. Understanding it takes about five minutes and will permanently change how you evaluate these parts.

What Is Shore A Hardness?

Shore A hardness measures the resistance of a rubber or elastomeric material to permanent indentation under a standardised blunt probe. The scale runs from 0 (no resistance — liquid) to 100 (full resistance — rigid). For truck suspension rubber components, the relevant range is roughly 40–80°.

The measurement is taken with a durometer — a handheld instrument with a spring-loaded needle that presses into the rubber surface under a defined force. A higher number means stiffer rubber. A lower number means softer, more compliant rubber.

Why the Correct Hardness Matters

The hollow spring — part 20390836 on Volvo FH/FM/FMX trucks — is designed to perform two functions simultaneously: load bearing and vibration isolation. These two requirements pull against each other in rubber compound design.

A harder rubber compound (higher Shore A) can bear more static load without deforming permanently — good for load capacity. But a harder compound also transmits more of the road's dynamic inputs directly through to the chassis — bad for vibration isolation.

A softer compound (lower Shore A) damps vibration well but may creep (deform permanently) under sustained axle load — which causes ride height to drop over time and puts the suspension geometry out of specification.

The Volvo OEM specification for 20390836 sits at 55–65° Shore A because this is the zone where the compound provides sufficient load-bearing capacity for the front axle of a loaded FH or FM truck while retaining enough compliance to absorb the 20–200 Hz road inputs that cause driver fatigue and cab hardware loosening on long-haul routes.

How Cheap Copies Cut Corners

Manufacturing a rubber compound at 55–65° Shore A requires precise formulation: specific grades of natural rubber, correct accelerator chemistry, measured vulcanisation time and temperature, and quality-controlled filler ratios. This costs money and requires process discipline.

Low-cost aftermarket producers reduce manufacturing complexity by using harder rubber compounds — typically 72–82° Shore A. A harder compound is more forgiving of process variation, requires less precision in vulcanisation control, and can be produced faster. It also looks physically identical to an OEM-spec part until you press a durometer against it.

The consequences in service:

  • Vibration transmission — a 75° Shore A hollow spring transmits roughly 2–3× more chassis vibration than a 60° Shore A part. The driver notices immediately. Fleet managers notice it in warranty claims and driver retention.
  • Bearing wear acceleration — the unabsorbed vibration loads that should dissipate in the hollow spring instead travel through the axle mounting stack to the hub bearings. Bearing replacement intervals shorten significantly.
  • Secondary hardware fatigue — brackets, air line clips, ABS sensor mounts, and chassis crossmember bolts all experience higher cyclic stress. This accelerates fatigue cracks and loosening.
  • False economy — a hollow spring that costs 40% less but fails at 60% of OEM service life is already more expensive per kilometre. Add secondary damage and unplanned downtime, and the true cost is multiples of the saving.

Can You Test Shore A Hardness in a Workshop?

Yes — a Shore A durometer costs approximately ₹2,000–5,000 from industrial suppliers and takes 10 seconds to use. Press the probe firmly and flatly against the flat face of the rubber cylinder (not the curved side, which gives inaccurate readings). A reading on a new OEM-spec part should fall between 55 and 65. A reading above 70 on a new part is a red flag.

You can also do a rough comparative test without instruments: press your thumb firmly into the flat face of the rubber. OEM-spec rubber should indent noticeably under firm thumb pressure. If the rubber resists your thumb without visible indentation, it is likely above 70° Shore A. This is not a calibrated test, but it catches the worst-case copies immediately.

The Vulcanisation Bond: The Other Failure Point

The rubber-to-steel bonding is the second major quality differentiator between OEM-spec and low-grade hollow springs. The steel plate inserts must be chemically bonded to the rubber compound through vulcanisation — not simply press-fitted or adhesive-bonded.

A proper vulcanised bond means the rubber and steel cure together under heat and pressure, creating a molecular-level interface that can withstand the cyclic shear forces of millions of suspension deflections. An adhesive or mechanical interface fails under the same cyclic loading — the steel insert begins to move independently, creating noise, destroying the spring's geometry, and eventually separating completely.

Visually, early bond failure shows as a thin crack at the rubber-steel interface around the outer edge of the insert — visible with a torch. A properly bonded new part has a seamless rubber surface flush to the edge of the steel plate with no visible interface line.
